VERDICT: The charter fails the user's own standard ("ABSOLUTELY AND WITHOUT COMPROMISE"). CC1–CC9 name the right safeguards, but the same 1–3 people who will sell the product run every one of them. They write the lock, hold the condition key, apply rater exclusions, unblind, choose the preregistration route and its fallback, assign evidence labels, and write the conclusions. CC4 makes even the independent statistician optional ("ideally"). Metascience is clear on why that matters. Industry-sponsored studies more often have favourable results (RR 1.27) and favourable conclusions (RR 1.34), and that bias "cannot be explained by standard 'Risk of bias' assessments" (Lundh et al., Cochrane 2017, via PubMed). The bias does not come through broken randomisation or blinding, which CC2/CC4 cover well. It comes through four routes: which questions and comparators are chosen, flexibility in the pilot and the analysis, which studies get published, and how results are interpreted. CC1–CC9 leave all four in the conflicted team's hands. Disclosure (CC6) is a floor, not a safeguard, and can make advice more biased (Cain, Loewenstein & Moore 2005). Two gaps are fatal: (1) no outside party controls the unblinding, the primary analysis or full data access; (2) the Registered Report is optional, and the conflicted team decides when to fall back, possibly after the pilot. Both can be fixed cheaply for a small team. The fixes are an independent statistician who holds the label key in escrow and publishes their own results report; PCI RR chosen before the pilot with no fallback left to the team (PCI RR is free, and its scheduled review shortens Stage 1); a public register of every study with a 12-month deadline for posting results; a results-to-claims table and a product-response table written into Stage 1; claim labels the system computes; a paid red team; an ICMJE-scope COI and AI-use disclosure; and putting the ADR log under public version control (the ActAI folder is currently not a git repository, so CC6's 'public provenance' cannot be audited). Rough total: under about $15k plus process time (my estimate, unverified except where cited). The real cost is giving outsiders control over timing and wording. ## [fatal] Self-administered independence. The people who profit from the product write the analysis code, hold the arm-label map, apply the preregistered rater exclusions, decide when to unblind and write the conclusions. 'Blind analysis on scrambled labels' is self-blinding: the harness that hashes the labels is theirs, so the blind holds only as long as they choose. Allegiance alone predicts outcomes (r = .26 across 30 meta-analyses of psychotherapy research; Munder et al. 2013), and sponsor bias survives good randomisation and blinding (Lundh et al. 2017). In 31 of 44 industry trials studied by Gøtzsche et al., the ghost author was the statistician. Control of the analysis is the sponsor's main lever.
- GAP: CC4 says an independent statistician 'ideally' signs off. Nothing names a party outside the team with custody of the key, full data access or veto power. CC2 locks the plan, but the lock-holder is the conflicted team. No ICMJE full-access/guarantor statement. No independent data custodian.
- FIX: Make independence a gate, not an aspiration. (1) Before Stage 1, contract a named independent statistician: no equity, no product role, and either no authorship or a guarantor role declared up front. (2) Key escrow: at generation time the harness encrypts the arm-label map to the statistician's public key, so the team never holds the plaintext. (3) The analysis script is frozen, run on simulated data, and its hash posted before the key is released. (4) The statistician receives the frozen Parquet data, applies the preregistered exclusions, runs the confirmatory script, deposits the data and their own signed results report on OSF/Zenodo with a DOI, and only then do the authors draft the Discussion. Any gap between that report and the paper is a declared deviation. (5) A non-conflicted guarantor (the statistician, or a co-investigator with no product stake) signs ICMJE's 'I had full access to all of the data' statement. (6) Get an independent CODECHECK certificate (with DOI) for the analysis code to reach TOP 2025 Level 3 ('a party independent from the researchers verified...').
- ENF: external_party | COST: Statistician: my estimate is 3–8 consulting days, about $3k–8k (unverified). Key escrow: about 1 day of harness work (age/GPG). CODECHECK is run by volunteers and lists no fee. The main cost is giving up control of the unblinding date and of the first published numbers.

## [fatal] Escape hatch in the pre-commitment. CC1 lets the team drop the Registered Report for an OSF preregistration 'if the RR timeline breaks the model-retirement window'. The conflicted team judges that condition, and the draft does not say whether it is judged before or after the all-arm pilot, when per-arm win rates will be visible. The strongest protection becomes optional just when the team learns whether it will be needed. OSF registrations can also be embargoed for up to four years, and an embargoed registration has to be made public before it can be withdrawn. A weak study could sit privately with no public trace. Some RR journals also let Stage 1 protocols sit under private embargo until Stage 2.
- GAP: CC1's fallback clause has no objective trigger, no decision date relative to the pilot, and no rule against embargoes. The ADR README still lists the route as an open decision.
- FIX: Decide the route now, before any pilot ratings exist, and record it as an ADR. Choose PCI RR and use its scheduled-review track: per Chambers 2021, Stage 1 reviews arrive about a week after full submission. If any fallback stays, write its trigger as an objective, dated condition (e.g. 'no IPA by <date>'). Make the fallback an OSF registration that is public immediately (no embargo), states that an RR was attempted, and posts the Stage 1 reviews. Sign the withdrawn-registration consent, so that withdrawing after IPA leaves a public summary. Handle model-retirement risk with the archive and the retirement contingency the plan already includes, not by switching routes.
- ENF: external_party | COST: $0: PCI RR is free to authors. Confirmatory generation waits weeks for Stage 1 review. Prompts, model IDs and briefs may have to be re-frozen if a model retires during review.

## [major] The pilot is unprotected, and only the team's own arm gets tuned. The all-arm pilot on the 12 development briefs comes before the lock. The team, unblinded, iterates prompts, calibrates the critic, sees per-arm win rates, and then chooses the smallest effect of interest, the hypothesis families, which ablations to keep or defer, and the final prompts. The protocol's authors tune C1-H; no one has an incentive to tune B-Plain, B-Random or B-Skill. This is the ML version of a sponsor choosing a weak comparator. When carefully tuned, standard LSTM baselines outperformed newer architectures (Melis et al. 2018). Stage 1 reviewers will see the pilot data, not the forking paths taken during it.
- GAP: The lock in CC1/CC2 comes after the pilot. Nothing governs who may see interim (pilot) arm effects; SPIRIT 2025 item 28b asks who will have access to interim results. No rule requires equal tuning effort across arms. ADR-0034 adds 'calibrate the critic once in the pilot', but the critic is calibrated by the team.
- FIX: (1) Pilot firewall, the data-monitoring-committee model applied to the pilot: pilot ratings go to the independent statistician, who returns only variance components (brief, rater and output SDs), timings, gold-check pass rates and costs, never arm-level effects. (2) Freeze baseline prompts before the pilot: B-Random verbatim from the article, B-Skill at a pinned commit, B-Plain as fixed text. C1-H may change only to fix failures from a predeclared list, each change logged as a diff with a reason. (3) A symmetric tuning budget: if C1-H gets N iterations, every baseline gets N, or nobody gets any. Publish the full prompt-change log in Stage 1. (4) Declare the pilot as pre-existing data and keep it out of every confirmatory test. In the protocol, give the one-line SPIRIT 28a reason why no full DMC is needed.
- ENF: process | COST: A few statistician hours. Slower prompt iteration. Little money.
- SRC: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-081477; https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05589; https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.MR000033.pub3

## [major] Spin and selective emphasis in the paper. With two co-primary outcomes (quality, distinctiveness), many secondaries, an expert panel and AI judges, the incentive is to headline whichever outcome won ('C1 makes pages more distinctive'), bury a quality loss or null ablations, and let 'C1-H' drift into 'C1'. Boutron et al. 2010 found spin in the abstract conclusions of 58% of trials with a non-significant primary outcome. In ML prediction studies, 20 of 21 abstracts that recommended clinical use had no external validation (Andaur Navarro et al. 2023). COMPare found outcome misreporting in 87% of trials in five top journals. Lundh et al. found results and conclusions agree less often in industry-sponsored studies (RR 0.83).
- GAP: CC7 covers product claims, not the paper. There is no rule on abstract or conclusion wording, no fixed outcome table, no rule that both co-primaries are always reported together, and no outside check of the conclusions beyond what the venue happens to do.
- FIX: (1) In Stage 1, preregister a results-to-claims table: for each outcome pattern (H1 win/null/loss × distinctiveness win/null/loss × each ablation TOST-equivalent/inconclusive/superior), the exact sentence the abstract will use. (2) Use a fixed table shell listing every registered outcome in registered order. The abstract reports both co-primaries with effect sizes and CIs. (3) Call the arm 'C1-H' everywhere. (4) Before submission, the independent statistician and one red-team member check the abstract, press text, README and launch posts against the table. PCI RR Stage 2 reviewers separately assess whether the conclusions are justified given the data. (5) Publish a pre-submission spin self-audit, using Boutron's categories or SPIN-PM.
- ENF: external_party | COST: About a day to write the table. Then minutes per publication.

## [major] Nobody is paid to find what is wrong. The protocol's authors are its proponents and its sellers. Normal peer review is unpaid, brief, and arrives after the design is set. Allegiance bias is substantial and robust (Munder et al. 2013), and a team of 1–3 cannot review itself.
- GAP: CC4 has no red team, no skeptic and no adversarial collaborator. The only outside critics are the venue's reviewers.
- FIX: (a) A paid red team before Stage 1 submission and again before Stage 2: 3–5 outside methodologists, a base fee plus a bounty per verified error, with a neutral arbiter, and the report published. This follows the Coles/Lakens precedent: 5 members, $200 base plus $100 per error, about 12 hours each, 6 critical errors found, $660 paid out. (b) Invite a known skeptic of prompt-protocol claims as an adversarial collaborator who co-signs the predictions and the claims table in Stage 1. This follows Melloni et al. 2023, where opposing theorists jointly preregistered contrasting predictions and theory-impartial labs ran the study.
- ENF: external_party | COST: Red team: about $1k–3k per round (scaled from the precedent; my estimate). Adversarial collaborator: a share of authorship and slower decisions.
