# methods

## Summary
Bottom line: Paper 1 should be a preregistered, fully crossed experiment. About 48 frozen product briefs are each run under about 10 conditions (C1-auto, plain prompt, "be creative", a compute-matched best-of-N, and about 6 leave-one-out ablations), with 2 independent generation runs per cell. Every page goes through one screenshot pipeline and is judged blind. The main human signal is lay Prolific raters making pairwise forced choices against full C1 on the same brief. One rater pool judges preference and a separate pool judges distinctiveness. A small expert panel gives CAT-style originality and craft ratings on a stratified subset. Held-out AI judges from other model families and embedding-based diversity metrics are secondary only, and are validated against a human pilot first. Analysis uses a Bradley-Terry mixed-effects logistic model with crossed random effects for rater, brief and output (maximal structure per Barr et al.). Ratings get ordinal CLMMs. Baselines and ablations are Holm-corrected families behind a gate, with TOST equivalence tests for any 'this block does nothing' claim, and Bayesian partial pooling as a robustness check. Power comes from the number of briefs, not raters. My simulation (brief SD 0.5 logits, rater SD 0.3): 8 briefs reach only about 0.43 power for a 60/40 preference even with 640 judgments, while 40 briefs x 20 judgments per contrast reach about 0.95. Ablation effects of 55/45 need about 60 to 150 briefs. So preregister a 58 to 60% smallest effect of interest (SESOI) for ablations, and run a pilot to estimate the variance components. Lay rating is cheap: about $3k for the main study at $15/h including Prolific's 42.8% corporate fee. Generation compute and expert time dominate the budget. Ethics: get an IRB exempt determination, preferably through an academic partner, which also lowers the Prolific fee to 33.3%. Venues: the CHI 2027 deadline (Sep 10, 2026) has passed, so target CHI 2028 (about Sep 2027) for Paper 1, with TMLR as the ML-audience fallback. Paper 2 (C3/C6a/C2) fits ACL/EMNLP via ARR, or a PNAS Nexus Registered Report. Nature Human Behaviour is a stretch.

## Recommendations
### Core experimental architecture (within vs between subjects) [high]
Use a fully crossed stimulus design. Freeze 48 briefs: 6 product categories x 8, fictional brand names, chosen by a preregistered sampling procedure. Generate every condition for every brief with 2 independent runs. Rating is within-rater: each rater judges many briefs and contrasts, sees each brief-contrast at most once, and sessions are about 15 min (about 30 pairs). Conditions are within-brief. Use between-subjects designs only where the treatment is applied to a person, for example a later study of whether operators using the app produce better pages, or where learning or contamination is unavoidable. Never use them for rating AI outputs.
- Why: Briefs and outputs are random stimulus factors. With crossed participants and stimuli, power plateaus as raters are added and is capped by the number of stimuli (Westfall, Kenny & Judd 2014). In NLP human evaluations, 69% used 100 or fewer items and 57% used 3 annotations per item, and these designs are underpowered (Card et al. 2020). Within-rater pairing cancels out rater leniency, and within-brief generation cancels out brief difficulty.
- Src: https://forrt.org/curated_resources/statistical-power-and-optimal-design-in/; https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.745/

### Arms: what C1 is compared against, and how human checkpoints are handled [high]
The confirmatory arm is 'C1-auto'. Every ?H checkpoint is replaced by a preregistered rule: initial pick keeps all 4 seeds, and the final pick is the in-loop critic's top rank or a random draw. D3 (CUT/TELLS/VOICE) is either run by an agent with frozen protocol prompts or dropped, depending on your decision. RELAY is excluded from Paper 1 because raters see static screenshots. Baselines: (1) plain prompt, (2) 'be creative' prompt, (3) compute-matched best-of-N: plain prompt sampled N times with the same token budget as C1 and selected by the same critic. Leave-one-out ablations: -SEED, -CRITIC loop, -blind critic (critic sees code), -stronger critic (critic is the same model as the worker), -ASSET, -D3. Human-in-the-loop C1 is a separate exploratory arm, with 4 or more operators modeled as a random factor.
- Why: The first reviewer objection will be that C1 simply spends more compute and selects harder. A compute-matched best-of-N with the same selector isolates the effect of seeding and critique. Human picks and voice passes add operator variance and allow experimenter influence. Si et al. separated LLM generation from human reranking as distinct conditions for the same reason.
- Src: https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04109v1

### Judgment formats: pairwise vs absolute vs ranking [high]
Lay raters (primary outcome): forced-choice pairwise (2AFC, no tie button) between full C1 and one comparison condition on the same brief. Wording is fixed and preregistered. Split two outcomes into separate rater pools so preference cannot spill over (halo) into distinctiveness: Pool A answers 'Which would you rather use / which do you prefer?'; Pool B answers 'Which looks less generic / template-like?'. Experts: CAT-style 7-point ratings of originality and craft; they view all outputs for a brief before rating, which makes the ratings relative. Best-Worst Scaling on 4-tuples is an alternative. Quality floor: VisAWI-S (4 items) as an absolute non-inferiority check. Do not use long Likert batteries as the primary outcome.
- Why: Pairwise judgments match the claim 'preferred', avoid scale-use differences between raters, and analyze cleanly with Bradley-Terry models (Chatbot Arena; the comparative-judgement meta-analysis found median SSR .91 across 101 sessions). With equal annotation counts, Best-Worst Scaling is more reliable than rating scales. RankME's relative judgments raised ICC from .12 to .42 for naturalness and from .41 to .68 for quality, compared with Likert. Absolute ratings are still needed, because a page can win pairwise while both options are poor. VisAWI is a validated, free website-aesthetics scale with 18 items, or 4 in the short form.
- Src: https://aclanthology.org/P17-2074/; https://arxiv.org/html/1803.05928; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12246014/; https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04132v1; https://aupsy.uni-wuppertal.de/en/instrumente/visawi/

### Outcome constructs and automated metrics (avoid circularity) [medium]
H1 (primary): lay preference, C1-auto vs plain. Co-primary, gated behind H1: lay distinctiveness. Secondary automated outcomes: (a) mean image-embedding distance of each output from a corpus of plain-prompt outputs; (b) within-condition diversity (Vendi score) across runs and briefs, as a homogenization measure. Both use a preregistered embedding model that is not used anywhere in the loop. Two outcomes are forbidden: the in-loop critic's scores, and hit counts against the protocol's own TELLS catalogue. Use a held-out tells list instead. Optional exploratory measure: a 5-second test of whether target users can name the intended feeling.
- Why: Doshi & Hauser measured collective homogenization as cosine similarity of embeddings, alongside human ratings. The Vendi score works with any similarity function and needs no reference distribution. Scoring a treatment with the same instrument it optimizes against is circular, and reviewers will flag it.
- Src: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532; https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02410

### Analysis models [high]
Confirmatory model: a mixed-effects logistic (Bradley-Terry) model per contrast, chose_C1 ~ 1 + left_position + trial_index + (1|rater) + (1|brief) + (1|output_pair), fit with lme4::glmer. The intercept is the log-odds that C1 wins; report win-rate with 95% CI. For a joint ranking of all conditions, use a difference-coded BT design matrix, with rater-by-condition random slopes where estimable. Follow Barr's 'keep it maximal' rule, and preregister the order in which terms are simplified if the model fails to converge: drop correlations first, then the smallest-variance slopes. Ratings: cumulative-link mixed models (ordinal::clmm or brms cumulative family). Never run t-tests on Likert means. Robustness checks: (a) two-way cluster bootstrap over raters and briefs; (b) a Bayesian hierarchical BT model in brms/Stan with partial pooling across ablations. Thurstone (probit) and BT (logit) give practically identical results; use BT for its tooling and familiarity.
- Why: Random-intercept-only models gave 40 to 50% false-positive rates in within-item designs (Barr et al. 2013). Card et al. recommend mixed models, and suggest mixed-effects logistic models for head-to-head comparisons. Treating ordinal data as metric distorts effect sizes and error rates (Burkner & Vuorre 2019). Chatbot Arena moved from Elo to BT for statistical estimation, using sandwich or bootstrap CIs. Multilevel models handle multiple comparisons through partial pooling (Gelman et al.).
- Src: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3881361; https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/ampps/2515245918823199/; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.06595; https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04132v1; https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.2478

### Power analysis and sample size (with numbers) [medium]
Size the study by simulation, not by formula. Treat these numbers as planning anchors. Independent two-sided binomial tests at 80% power need these numbers of judgments: 55/45 -> 783; 58/42 -> 304; 60/40 -> 194; 65/35 -> 85. At a Holm-worst alpha of .05/6: 1,208 / 470 / 300 / 132. Clustering by brief dominates. In my Monte Carlo (brief SD 0.5 logit, rater SD 0.3, analysis at brief level), a 60/40 effect gives: 8 briefs x 80 judgments = 0.43 power; 24 x 20 = 0.79; 40 x 20 = 0.95. For ablations at alpha .0083: a 60/40 effect gives 40 x 20 = 0.85 and 60 x 20 = 0.96. A 55/45 effect gives only 0.44 at 60 x 40 (0.80 if brief SD is 0.3), and needs about 100 to 150 briefs. Plan: (1) Pilot with 8 briefs x all conditions x 1 run, about 60 raters x 30 pairs. (2) Fit the confirmatory model and extract the brief, output and rater variance components. (3) Rerun the simulation in the DeBruine & Barr style, targeting 90% power for H1 and 80% for each ablation at a preregistered SESOI of 58 to 60%. Default main study: 48 briefs, 2 runs per cell, 20 or more judgments per brief per contrast per outcome pool. If you choose a Registered Report, plan for power of at least 0.95 (Nature Human Behaviour) or at least 0.90 (PNAS Nexus).
- Why: Effect sizes in the literature are small to moderate. Model-vs-baseline differences in human evaluations had an SD of .12 on a 0-1 scale; 3 workers x 100 items detects only effects of 0.2 or more in high-variance settings (Card et al.). Five GenAI ideas raised story novelty by 8.1% (Doshi & Hauser: 600 evaluators, 9 to 14 ratings per story). AI ideas scored 5.64 vs 4.84 for humans on novelty (Si et al.: 79 reviewers, 298 reviews). Simulation is the recommended way to power crossed designs (DeBruine & Barr 2021). The variance values in my simulation are assumptions until the pilot replaces them.
- Src: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.06595; https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/223488; https://forrt.org/curated_resources/statistical-power-and-optimal-design-in/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532; https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04109v1; https://media.nature.com/original/nature-cms/uploads/ckeditor/attachments/4127/RegisteredReportsGuidelines_NatureHumanBehaviour.pdf; https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/pages/registered-reports-guidelines

### Multiple-comparison control for baselines and ablations [high]
Use hierarchical gatekeeping. Family 1: H1 (C1-auto vs plain, preference) at alpha .05. Only if H1 passes, test Family 2 with Holm correction: C1 vs 'be creative', C1 vs compute-matched best-of-N, and distinctiveness vs plain. Family 3: about 6 leave-one-out ablations vs full C1, Holm-corrected. Any claim that a block 'adds nothing' requires a TOST equivalence test with preregistered bounds, for example a win-rate between 45% and 55%. A non-significant result alone does not support that claim. The quality floor (VisAWI-S) is a one-sided non-inferiority test with a preregistered margin. Report Bayesian hierarchical estimates with a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) as secondary. Label interactions such as category x condition as exploratory.
- Why: Ablations are many-to-one comparisons and invite cherry-picking, and reviewers expect a declared correction (the Nature Human Behaviour RR guidelines explicitly require 'appropriate correction for multiple comparisons'). TOST lets you statistically reject effects larger than the SESOI. Partial pooling is a principled complement.
- Src: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5502906; https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.2478; https://media.nature.com/original/nature-cms/uploads/ckeditor/attachments/4127/RegisteredReportsGuidelines_NatureHumanBehaviour.pdf

### Inter-rater reliability: what to compute and how to read it [medium]
For pairwise data, report: (a) split-half reliability of per-output BT scores across random halves of raters, Spearman-Brown corrected, over 100 or more splits; (b) scale separation reliability (SSR); (c) mean agreement with the majority choice per pair; (d) Krippendorff's alpha (nominal) with a bootstrap CI. For ratings, report Krippendorff's alpha (ordinal) and ICC(2,k) with bootstrap CIs and q(alpha < alpha_min) for alpha_min = .667 / .80 (Hayes & Krippendorff 2007). Expect low pairwise agreement on subjective taste: Si et al. found 56% between reviewers, and UICrit designers reached Fleiss' kappa of about .3. Treat this as information about the task, not as grounds to exclude raters. Report lay and expert results as separate constructs, and give condition-level agreement between the panels. Never pool them. Crowd-expert agreement in Chatbot Arena was about 73%, against 79 to 90% between experts.
- Why: Ten expert poets reached alpha of .83, while novices fell to .575 when standardized to the same 10 raters, and the expert-novice correlation was only .21 (Kaufman et al. 2008). Expert and lay judgments measure different things. In the comparative-judgement meta-analysis, split-half reliability was lower than SSR (median .79 vs .91), and about 10 comparisons per item usually suffices for SSR of .80 or more. Krippendorff's alpha handles any number of raters, missing data and any level of measurement.
- Src: https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2021-03/Answering%20the%20Call%20for%20a%20Standard%20Reliability%20Measure%20for%20Coding%20Data.pdf; https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2021-03/Computing%20Krippendorff%27s%20Alpha-Reliability.pdf; https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/CATRatersPoems.pdf; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12246014/; https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08850v3; https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04132v1

### Prolific recruitment, pay and quality control [high]
Pay $15/h. That is above Prolific's recommended £9/$12; the minimum is £6/$8. Set pay from timed pilot sessions and top up anyone underpaid. Filters: approval rate of 98% or more, fluent English stated explicitly (it is not guaranteed by default), a fixed country set, and an in-study desktop-only check plus a self-reported colour-vision item. Put a comprehension check at the start, allow 2 attempts, and ask those who fail to return the study rather than rejecting them. Include 2 instructional manipulation checks plus 3 'gold' catch pairs, each a deliberately broken page against a polished one. Preregister data-exclusion rules, kept separate from payment decisions: failing 2 or more gold or attention items, median trial time under 3 s, or choosing the same side more than 90% of the time. Reject payments only under Prolific's rule: at least 2 failed attention checks in studies of 5 minutes or longer. Use image-only stimuli, tell participants not to use AI tools, and turn on Prolific authenticity checks if any free text is collected. Use completion codes through the Prolific API.
- Why: Prolific's policy limits how checks may be used for rejection. Telling participants directly not to use AI cut AI use from 10.9% to 7.1%, according to Prolific's own guidance. Prolific and CloudResearch gave the most high-quality respondents: about 68% on Prolific vs 26% on MTurk, at $1.90 per high-quality respondent. Note that a Gmail account pays the 42.8% corporate fee; the 33.3% academic/non-profit fee requires an institutional email.
- Src: https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/article/2273bd; https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445153-prolific-s-attention-and-comprehension-check-policy; https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445207-how-do-i-prevent-ai-generated-responses-in-my-study.md; https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445159-how-do-i-use-prolific-s-filters.md; https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/article/9cd998; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013894/

### Expert panels [medium]
Recruit 8 to 10 professional designers with at least 3 years of experience, verified portfolios and no conflicts of interest. Prolific's Domain Experts pool has no design category, so use design communities or freelance marketplaces, or Prolific custom screening with portfolio checks. Run them under the Consensual Assessment Technique (CAT): ratings are independent with no conferring, there is no rubric beyond short definitions of originality and craft, and each brief's full set of outputs is viewed before rating. Give them a stratified subset, for example 16 briefs x all conditions x 1 run, which is about 1.5 to 2 hours per expert. Budget about $75 to 100/h; this is my market estimate, not verified. Si et al. paid $25 per review. Report expert demographics and experience.
- Why: CAT validity rests on panels of domain experts; reliabilities typically exceed .70 with around 10 judges, and novices cannot substitute for them (Kaufman et al.). Expert preference is the defensible referent for 'creative' or 'distinctive'. Lay preference is the referent for 'audience appeal'.
- Src: https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/CATRatersPoems.pdf; https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445228-domain-experts.md; https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04109v1

### Held-out AI judges and automated metrics: role and validation [high]
AI judges are secondary outcomes only. Use at least 2 judges from model families different from both the worker and the in-loop critic; if the loop uses Claude, use GPT-5.x and Gemini. Never use the loop critic as a judge. Run them pairwise in both orders (position swap) and count only order-consistent verdicts. Freeze the temperature and prompts, and preregister them. Before using a judge, validate it against the pilot's human judgments for each outcome and domain. Report agreement, position bias and self-family bias. Do not report a judge for an outcome where it does not beat a preregistered agreement threshold.
- Why: LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases (Zheng et al.). Their reliability varies with task and property, so they must be validated against humans per task (Bavaresco et al., ACL 2025). For creative-writing quality, no LLM judge correlated positively with expert TTCW assessments (Chakrabarty et al., CHI 2024).
- Src: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685; https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18403v2; https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14556v2

### Blinding, randomization and stimulus standardization [medium]
Rendering: one pipeline for every arm. Use the same viewports (1440x900 desktop, 390 mobile, fold crop), the same font loading and wait-for-network-idle, a fixed cap on full-page height, and remove all model or tool marks. Brands: within a brief, every condition uses the same fictional brand name and copy constraints. Display: randomize left/right placement and trial order, and log both as covariates. Generation: interleave runs across conditions in random order within a short time window, and record model snapshot IDs and timestamps to guard against API drift. Analyst blinding: condition labels stay hashed until the analysis code is frozen and has been run on simulated data. Distinctiveness pool only: show a short neutral calibration gallery of generic outputs from held-out briefs. Instructions never mention the hypothesis.
- Why: Style is the outcome, so the style-normalization trick Si et al. used for blinding is unavailable. Blinding has to come from standardized rendering and neutral labels instead. Crowd raters judged better when they could compare against references (Karpinska et al. 2021). Raters favour one screen side, and API models change over time; both are confounds you can avoid.
- Src: https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.97/; https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04109v1

### Preregistration [high]
Register the main study on OSF using the full OSF Preregistration template. Do this after freezing briefs, prompts, model IDs and the rendering pipeline, and before collecting any human rating data. Declare any stimuli generated beforehand and any pilot data, and keep pilot data out of confirmatory tests. Register: hypotheses H1 to H3 and their families; arms, with the exact prompts and seeds policy; the brief sampling frame; runs per cell and stop rules; outcome wording; rater population and filters; N together with the power simulation code and stopping rule; exclusion rules; model formulas, random-effects structure and the order for simplifying it; the multiplicity plan; SESOIs, equivalence and non-inferiority bounds; the AI-judge validation protocol; and which analyses are exploratory. Attach a completed HEDS sheet. Log every deviation in a 'Transparent Changes' document. AsPredicted's 8-question form is enough for small side studies. For Paper 2, consider a PNAS Nexus Registered Report for in-principle acceptance.
- Why: Preregistration is the standard defence against HARKing in HCI (Cockburn et al.) and in NLP (van Miltenburg et al., NAACL 2021 best thematic paper). Doshi & Hauser's Science Advances study was preregistered on AsPredicted. HEDS records human-evaluation details in enough detail for reproducibility. A PNAS Nexus Registered Report requires power of 0.9 or more for every test.
- Src: https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg; https://aspredicted.org/wy59x.pdf; https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06944; https://forrt.org/curated_resources/hark-no-more-on-the-preregistration-of-c/; https://aclanthology.org/2022.humeval-1.6; https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/pages/registered-reports-guidelines

### Ethics / IRB for crowdsourced ratings [medium]
Obtain a formal exemption determination; do not self-declare. Rating websites is very likely exempt under the 2018 Common Rule, as category 2 (survey procedures) or category 3 (benign behavioral intervention with adult consent), but institutions decide who may make that determination. Preferred route: an academic co-investigator's IRB. This also qualifies you for Prolific's 33.3% academic fee instead of 42.8%. Fallback: an independent or commercial IRB. Consent should cover the purpose, the fact that stimuli are AI-generated websites, duration, pay, the right to withdraw, and open release of anonymized ratings. Prolific IDs are pseudonymous, so re-key them before release. In the paper, report the approving body, the protocol ID and the consent procedure.
- Why: Prolific does not mandate IRB approval but provides documentation for applications. The venues do require ethics information: ACL checklist D3-D4, NeurIPS checklist items 14-15, and ACM's human-participants policy, which CHI 2027 applies and which can lead to retraction. A 2026 audit found only 3.2% of human-subject papers at IEEE VIS reported approval, an approval ID and consent together.
- Src: https://research.uci.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018-categories-of-exempt-human-subjects-research.pdf; https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/449628-irb-applications-and-ethics-approval.md; https://chi2027.acm.org/?p=823; https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13295; https://aclrollingreview.org/responsibleNLPresearch/; https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist

### Open data and code norms [high]
Release everything with DOIs on OSF plus Zenodo, or on Hugging Face with Croissant and RAI metadata if you target the NeurIPS Evaluations & Datasets track. Include: briefs; prompts; seeds; model IDs and dates; generation logs with itemized costs; all screenshots and HTML; trial-level ratings with re-keyed rater IDs and coarse demographics; the exclusion log; analysis and power-simulation code; the preregistration and deviations; HEDS; and the rater instructions with screenshots of the rating UI. License data CC-BY-4.0 and code MIT/Apache-2.0. Before releasing, check model and image-generation output terms. Use fictional brands only.
- Why: PNAS Nexus Registered Reports require public raw data, materials and scripts; 'available on request' is not enough. The NeurIPS 2026 E&D track requires Croissant with RAI metadata and hosted, executable code. Card et al. call for sharing raw human-evaluation data and analysis code. CHI expects supplementary materials that support reproducibility.
- Src: https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/pages/registered-reports-guidelines; https://nips.cc/Conferences/2026/EvaluationsDatasetsFAQ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.06595; https://chi2027.acm.org/?p=2598

### Reporting checklists [high]
Have the harness auto-draft every checklist from the study record. ACL Responsible NLP checklist: A1-A2 limitations and risks; B1-B6 artifacts and licenses; C1-C4 compute and settings; D1-D5 full instructions, recruitment and pay, consent, ethics review, demographics; E1 AI assistant use. NeurIPS 16-item checklist, especially #7 error bars with their definition, #14 instructions, screenshots and pay at or above minimum wage, #15 IRB, and #16 declaring LLMs as a methodological component. CHI: 5,000 to 8,000 words, an ethics context note for reviewers, anonymized supplementary materials, and the ACM authorship policy on generative AI. Add HEDS and a CONSORT-style rater flow diagram (recruited -> excluded -> analyzed).
- Why: These are the venues' actual requirements. Missing human-evaluation details is the most common reproducibility failure in the field: 34% of experiments omitted ratings per item (Card et al.), and most crowd-evaluation papers omit task details (Karpinska et al.).

### What this implies for the app/harness (and the 'for dummies' runbook) [medium]
Make the 'Study Card' the unit the app is built around. A Study Card holds hypotheses, arms (configuration presets plus ablation toggles instead of raw block grammar), the brief set, judges (lay, expert or AI mix), N from the built-in power simulator, exclusions and the analysis. From one card the app produces: (1) a frozen generation manifest (prompts, model IDs, seeds), run with interleaving and an itemized cost preview before spending; (2) standardized renders; (3) a rater web app with pairwise/BWS/CAT modes, randomization, gold trials and Prolific completion codes; (4) exports of the OSF preregistration fields, HEDS, consent text, and drafts of the ACL/NeurIPS checklists; (5) R and Python analysis notebooks (lme4, ordinal, brms) that run unchanged on pilot and main data. The per-configuration 'for dummies' runbook is then a walkthrough of filling in and running a Study Card.
- Why: Methodological rigor gets enforced by the tool instead of remembered by the user. It is also what makes every configuration testable the same way across Paper 1 and Paper 2.
- Src: https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg; https://aclanthology.org/2022.humeval-1.6

### Human-evaluation budget sketch (to be replaced by the itemized preview) [low]
Lay main study: 9 contrasts x 48 briefs x 20 judgments x 2 outcome pools = 17,280 trials. At about 30 trials per 15-minute session that is about 576 sessions x $3.75 = $2,160, plus the 42.8% Prolific fee, about $3,085, plus VAT on the fee where it applies. Pilot: about $500. Expert panel: 8 x about 2-3 h x about $85/h (my estimate) = about $1.4k to $2k. Total human evaluation is about $5 to 6k plus 15% contingency. The generation side is 48 x 10 x 2 = 960 runs, measured per run in the pilot. Full C1-like runs (about 20 critic calls with images, about 20 worker turns, plus image generation) will likely dominate total cost.
- Why: Pay and fee figures come from Prolific's pages. Trial duration, expert rates and generation cost per run are assumptions until the pilot measures them.

## Options
### Primary judgment format for lay raters -> Pairwise 2AFC for lay raters (separate pools for preference and distinctiveness), plus VisAWI-S as an absolute quality floor; CAT ratings or BWS for experts
- Pairwise forced choice (2AFC) vs full C1 on the same brief: + Matches the 'preferred' claim; reliable without scale calibration; analyzed with BT and mixed logistic models; cheap per judgment | - Shows relative preference only (both options could be bad); needs a separate absolute quality floor
- Absolute Likert ratings per page: + Gives absolute levels; one page per trial | - Rater scale-use noise; lower reliability than relative methods (RankME, BWS evidence); needs ordinal models
- Best-Worst Scaling / ranking of 4-tuples: + More reliable than rating scales per annotation; efficient with many conditions | - Heavier cognitive load with full-page screenshots; analysis is less familiar to HCI reviewers
### How C1's human checkpoints (?H pick, CUT/TELLS/VOICE) enter Paper 1 -> C1-auto for the confirmatory test; an operator-pool arm as an exploratory or follow-up study
- C1-auto: replace ?H with preregistered rules and agent-executed D3: + No operator variance or experimenter bias; fully reproducible; cheap | - Departs from the article's human-finished workflow; D3 run by an agent is a deviation
- Blinded operator pool (4+ designers) as a random factor: + Tests the real human-in-the-loop workflow; generalizes across operators | - Expensive; operator skill is confounded with the protocol; needs many more operators for power
- Owner performs all checkpoints: + Easiest; closest to how the article was demonstrated | - Single unblinded operator who wants the result: reviewers will reject this as confirmatory evidence
### Baseline set -> All three baselines
- Plain prompt only: + Cheapest | - Gains can't be told apart from extra compute and selection
- Plain + 'be creative': + Tests the article's claim that variety must not come from 'be creative' | - Still open to the compute confound
- Plain + 'be creative' + compute-matched best-of-N selected by the same critic: + Isolates seeding and critique from more sampling and selection; heads off the main reviewer objection | - Adds one arm of generation and rating cost
### Confirmatory inference framework -> Frequentist mixed-effects BT as confirmatory, Bayesian hierarchical model as preregistered robustness or secondary analysis
- Frequentist mixed-effects BT/logistic (lme4) + Holm gatekeeping: + Familiar to CHI/ACL/NeurIPS reviewers; straightforward to preregister | - Convergence problems with maximal random effects; needs a preregistered order for simplifying the model
- Bayesian hierarchical BT (brms/Stan) with ROPE: + Partial pooling handles ablations naturally; can support 'no meaningful effect' claims | - Priors draw reviewer scrutiny; Registered Reports demand Bayes factors of 6 or 10 if used for inference
- Per-pair binomial or t-tests: + Simple | - Ignores rater and brief clustering, which inflates false positives (Barr et al.); seen as weak practice
### Ablation inference standard -> Holm + TOST, with Bayesian estimates reported alongside
- Holm-corrected significance tests only: + Simple | - Cannot support 'block X doesn't matter'; small effects are unpowered
- Holm + TOST equivalence with a preregistered SESOI (58-60%) and bounds (45-55%): + Supports both 'matters' and 'negligible' conclusions; honest about power | - Requires defending the SESOI; some results will be inconclusive
- Bayesian ROPE only: + Coherent estimation | - Less accepted as confirmatory evidence at HCI venues
### Number of briefs (stimulus sample) -> 48 briefs (6 categories x 8), adjusted after the pilot's variance estimates
- 24 briefs: + Cheaper generation | - Simulated power about 0.79 for a 60/40 H1 at 20 judgments per brief; ablations underpowered
- 48 briefs: + Power of about 0.95 or more for H1 and about 0.85 to 0.96 for 60/40 ablations under the assumed variances | - About twice the generation cost of 24
- 96+ briefs: + Needed only if you insist on detecting 55/45 ablation effects | - Generation cost roughly doubles again
### Primary judge population for H1 -> General lay raters for H1 (preference); experts as a separate preregistered secondary family for originality and craft
- General lay raters (fixed country set): + Cheap, scalable, matches 'audience appeal' | - May disagree with expert notions of originality (expert-novice r = .21 for poetry)
- Lay raters screened as target customers of each brief: + Ecologically closer to real buyers | - Fewer eligible raters; screening per brief is complex
- Professional designers: + Defensible for 'distinctive/creative' claims (CAT) | - Expensive; small N limits power for a primary test
### Preregistration vehicle -> OSF Preregistration for Paper 1; consider a PNAS Nexus Registered Report for Paper 2
- AsPredicted: + Fast, 8 questions | - Too thin for a multi-family ablation design
- OSF Preregistration (+ HEDS + power code): + Detailed, public, timestamped; recognized across venues | - More writing up front
- Registered Report (PNAS Nexus / Nature Human Behaviour): + In-principle acceptance; highest credibility | - Power of 0.90 or 0.95 required for every test; slow two-stage review; wrong venue fit for Paper 1
### Ethics review route -> Academic collaborator's IRB
- Academic collaborator's IRB: + Credible exemption determination; unlocks Prolific's 33.3% academic fee; adds an academic co-author | - Shared authorship; institutional timeline
- Independent or commercial IRB: + You keep full control | - Fee (unverified, likely four figures); still need to justify independent status to venues
- No formal review, self-assessed as exempt: + Fastest | - Fails ACL D4 / NeurIPS #15 / ACM policy expectations; retraction risk at ACM venues
### Rater pay rate -> $15 per hour, set from timed pilot sessions
- Prolific minimum (£6/$8 per hour): + Cheapest | - Reviewer and ethics criticism; worse attention
- Prolific recommended (£9/$12 per hour): + Meets the platform norm | - Only marginal headroom if sessions run long
- $15 per hour: + Ethical margin, better retention; adds only about $0.5k to the lay budget | - Slightly higher cost
### Paper 1 venue -> CHI 2028 with an arXiv preprint as soon as results are in; TMLR as the fallback
- CHI 2028 (about Sep 2027, inferred): + Top HCI venue; best fit for a design and creativity study with human evaluation; realistic timeline | - About a year away; highly competitive
- NeurIPS 2027 Evaluations & Datasets (about May 2027): + ML visibility; rewards the open harness plus the rated dataset | - Tight timeline; framing must center on evaluation or data, with Croissant/RAI work
- TMLR (rolling): + Soundness-based acceptance; no deadline pressure | - Less visible to the design and HCI audience
- DIS / C&C / UIST 2027: + Earlier publication; strong topical fit (C&C, DIS) | - Deadlines from Jan to Apr 2027 are too tight for a powered, preregistered study

## Risks
- Ablation effects are smaller than baseline effects and come out underpowered (simulated power of 0.44 to 0.80 at 60 briefs for 55/45 effects), so the paper ends up reporting ambiguous nulls => Preregister a 58-60% SESOI, use TOST equivalence, estimate variance components in a pilot and resize the study, add briefs before adding raters, and report Bayesian estimates alongside
- Compute confound: C1 wins only because it samples and selects more => Add a compute-matched best-of-N baseline with the same critic and token budget; log tokens and cost per run and report them
- Circular evaluation: the in-loop critic or the TELLS catalogue also serves as the outcome measure => Use only held-out, different-family AI judges and a held-out tells list; humans are the primary outcome
- Operator or experimenter bias from human checkpoints and owner involvement => Make C1-auto confirmatory; keep labels hashed until analysis code is frozen; run any human-in-the-loop arm with multiple blinded operators modeled as a random effect
- Model and API drift during generation (snapshot changes, rate limits favouring some arms) => Interleave conditions in random order within a short window; pin and record model IDs and timestamps; archive all outputs before rating
- Distinctiveness trades off against usability or first-impression appeal (prototypicality effects) => Preregister a VisAWI-S non-inferiority quality floor; keep preference and distinctiveness as separate outcomes in separate rater pools; consider the structure=conservative arm as exploratory
- Lay and expert panels disagree, making the headline claim ambiguous => Preregister them as distinct constructs (audience appeal vs expert originality), never pool them, and report both whatever the result
- Low-quality, bot or AI-assisted raters on Prolific => Image-only stimuli, gold catch pairs, attention checks within Prolific's rules, preregistered exclusions, explicit no-AI instruction, short desktop-only sessions
- No IRB route for an independent researcher, which blocks ACL, NeurIPS and ACM venues => Recruit an academic collaborator early, or budget for an independent IRB; report approval body, ID and consent
- Brief-selection researcher degrees of freedom (choosing briefs where C1 shines) => Preregister a sampling frame and random draw of briefs across categories; freeze the set before generating any stimuli
- Release problems: real brands or trademarks in outputs, image-generator licence terms, re-identifiable rater IDs => Fictional brands only; check provider output terms before release; re-key Prolific IDs and release coarse demographics only
- Reviewers dismiss the work as 'prompt engineering' with weak novelty => Frame the contribution as controlled causal evidence on countering AI homogenization, plus component ablations, plus an open, reusable harness and rated dataset; tie it to the Doshi & Hauser homogenization literature
- Venue dates marked as inferred shift => Re-verify deadlines when calls are published; keep TMLR (rolling) as the always-open fallback

## Questions
- For Paper 1's confirmatory arm, do you accept 'C1-auto'? That means human picks are replaced by preregistered rules, D3 (CUT/TELLS/VOICE) is either run by an agent or dropped, and RELAY is excluded. The human-finished workflow would then be tested later as a separate operator study. If not, who would the operators be?
- Who should be the primary judge for H1 'preferred': general lay raters in a fixed country set (my recommendation), lay raters screened as target customers of each brief, or professional designers?
- Do you have, or can you get, an academic collaborator whose IRB can review the study? It also cuts Prolific's fee from 42.8% to 33.3%. If not, are you willing to pay for an independent IRB?
- Are you willing to frame ablations around a 58-60% smallest effect of interest, accepting that 55/45-sized block contributions will come out as 'inconclusive'? The alternative is roughly doubling to 96+ briefs.
- Venue and timeline: should Paper 1 aim at CHI 2028 (about Sep 2027) for the best fit, or at a faster ML route (NeurIPS 2027 Evaluations & Datasets in about May 2027, or TMLR rolling)?
- Rater geography: US/UK only (simpler, consistent web conventions), or multinational to test cultural variation in visual preference? Reinecke & Gajos found preferences differ by country, gender and education.
- Pay: OK to pay raters $15/h (above Prolific's recommended $12/h) and experts about $75-100/h?
- Will you commit to releasing all stimuli, prompts, ratings and code publicly (CC-BY / MIT)? PNAS Nexus and the NeurIPS E&D track effectively require it, and it is the strongest credibility signal for an independent team.
